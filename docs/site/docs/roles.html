9. Roles and permissions

A permission is never held in the abstract. It is held by a role, the role is bound to an identity within a scope, and the scope says which part of the fleet the role reaches. This chapter is the catalogue of the six roles, what each may and may not do, how a scope narrows a binding, and what an operation asks for beyond a role.

The six roles

The catalogue is fixed: the panel knows these six roles and no others, and a request naming another is refused with unknown_role. A role is a list of permissions, not a rank — the lists overlap, but none of them contains another. The whole catalogue is served at GET /api/v1/roles, role by role with the permissions it carries.

RoleWhat it is forPermissions
viewerLooks at the fleet. Reads the hosts and their modules, and computes plans; changes nothing.31
auditorReviews the installation. Reads the audit trail, the metrics, the findings, the secret metadata and the state of the CA; fixes nothing.23
operatorIs on call. Restarts services, runs containers, plans upgrades, orders campaigns, makes backups, silences alerts; approves nothing and carries out no package transaction.63
approverIs the second person. Reads the change and the trail, approves operations and campaigns, publishes a policy; orders nothing.12
identity_adminDecides who has access to the hosts. The directory, its policies and the local accounts; runs no operations on hosts.24
platform_adminAdministers the installation. Holds every permission there is.187

An identity can hold several roles at once, each in a scope of its own: an on-call engineer is usually an operator across their site and a viewer across the rest of the fleet. The permissions add up, the scopes do not merge — a permission is held where the binding that carries it reaches, and nowhere else.

viewer

The role that reads. It sees the hosts and their modules, and it can ask the host what a change would come to, because a plan computes a difference and writes nothing.

What it may doPermissions
See the fleet, its inventory, its operations and its campaigns.host.read, inventory.read, job.read, campaign.read
Read the state of a host: services, processes, packages, storage, network, firewall, SSH, kernel, time, security, certificates, backups, containers, monitoring, vulnerabilities, accounts and the declared state.unit.status, process.read, packages.read, storage.read, storage.smart.read, network.read, dns.read, firewall.read, ssh.read, kernel.read, time.read, security.read, certificate.read, backup.read, docker.read, docker.events, monitoring.read, vulnerability.read, identity.read, localuser.read, policy.read
Compute a plan: what a change would come to on this host, without making it.dns.plan, time.plan, file.plan, kernel.module.plan, certificate.plan, certificate.trust.plan

What it may not do. Nothing that writes: no operation can be ordered, because job.create is not in the list. It does not read the audit trail either — that is the auditor's material, not a viewer's — nor the metrics, nor the secret metadata, and it cannot run a security scan: a scan collects reconnaissance material about the host and has a permission of its own.

auditor

The role that reviews. Its work is the trail, the findings and the state of the installation, and it is deliberately not the role that repairs any of them.

What it may doPermissions
See the fleet, its inventory, its operations and its campaigns.host.read, inventory.read, job.read, campaign.read
Read the evidence: the audit trail, the fleet metrics, the state of the CA and the metadata of the secrets. The value of a secret cannot be read through the API at all, by anybody.audit.read, metrics.read, pki.read, secret.read
Read the material of a review, and run the scan that produces it: the protective state, the vulnerabilities, the installed packages behind them, the certificate dates, the backups, the alerts and silences, and the verdicts of the declared state.security.read, security.scan, vulnerability.read, packages.read, certificate.read, certificate.plan, certificate.trust.plan, backup.read, monitoring.read, policy.read
See who has access: the directory, its HBAC and sudo rules, the local accounts and the containers on a host.identity.read, identity.policy.read, localuser.read, docker.read, docker.events

What it may not do. It replaces nothing it reviews: not the CA (pki.rotate is the administrator's), not a finding (security.remediate), not a policy. It also does not hold the ordinary module reads of a host — network, storage, firewall, SSH, kernel, time — which is the one place where a viewer sees more than an auditor: an identity that needs both holds both roles.

operator

The on-call role, and the largest of the five that are not the administrator. Its shape is one decision repeated: everything that gets a service back up, nothing that changes what the host is.

What it may doPermissions
Order work and take it back. Refreshing the inventory is the first move in an outage: before changing a host, one wants to know how things are now.host.read, inventory.read, inventory.refresh, job.read, job.create, job.cancel
Move services and read the journal.unit.start, unit.stop, unit.restart, unit.reload, unit.reset_failed, journal.read
Run containers and pull images; plan a declaration or a project deployment without carrying it out.docker.read, docker.events, docker.container.logs, docker.container.start, docker.container.stop, docker.container.restart, docker.image.pull, docker.plan, docker.compose.plan
Plan a package transaction, without carrying it out.packages.read, packages.plan
Run and steer a campaign, and write the declared state as a draft. Publishing that draft is the approval, and it stays with the approver.campaign.read, campaign.create, campaign.control, policy.read, policy.write
Name the targets of a campaign and declare a host under repair.host.tag.write, host.group.write, host.maintenance.write
Invite a machine into the fleet, one token per host, see the pending installations and close a token that leaked.host.enroll.create, host.enroll.read, host.enroll.revoke
Read the local accounts, and move the boundary of an existing one: its groups and its expiry.localuser.read, localuser.groups.write, localuser.expiry.write
Read the configuration of a host and compute what a change would come to, without making it.network.read, dns.read, dns.plan, firewall.read, storage.read, storage.smart.read, ssh.read, kernel.read, kernel.module.plan, time.read, time.plan, security.read, security.scan, file.plan, vulnerability.read
Watch certificates and tell the panel which files to watch.certificate.read, certificate.plan, certificate.trust.plan, certificate.watch
Make a backup and verify it. Restoring is a separate decision, because it unpacks old state onto a running system.backup.read, backup.run, backup.verify
Read alerts, probe a host and silence an alert during a repair.monitoring.read, monitoring.probe, monitoring.silence.write, notification.read

What it may not do. It approves nothing: neither job.approve nor campaign.approve nor policy.publish is in the list, so an operator who orders a mutation waits for somebody else. It carries out no package transaction (packages.install, packages.remove, packages.upgrade, packages.repair) and does not replace the agent. It writes nothing on the configuration of a host: no network, no firewall, no storage, no SSH, no kernel, no time, no managed file — a bad change there cuts the host off and cannot be fixed remotely. It does not reboot or shut a host down, does not remove or prune a container, does not deploy a Compose project, does not restore a backup, does not create or delete a local account, and does not read the audit trail.

approver

The smallest role, and the one the second-person rule rests on. It reads the change, the trail and the people, and says yes.

What it may doPermissions
Read what it is being asked to approve, and the trail behind it.host.read, inventory.read, job.read, campaign.read, audit.read
Approve an operation or a campaign.job.approve, campaign.approve
Read and publish the declared state. Publishing a policy is the approval of a draft somebody else wrote.policy.read, policy.publish
See who has access: the directory, its HBAC and sudo rules, and the local accounts.identity.read, identity.policy.read, localuser.read

What it may not do. It orders nothing: there is no job.create, no campaign.create and no policy.write, so an approver cannot write the change they then approve. It reads no module of a host beyond the inventory, and it cancels nothing.

identity_admin

The role that decides who has access to the hosts — through the directory, or past it, through a local account. It does not run operations on hosts.

What it may doPermissions
See the fleet it is granting access to.host.read, inventory.read, job.read, campaign.read, unit.status, notification.read
Manage the directory: its users, its groups, its HBAC and sudo rules, the membership of a host, and the records the whole network reads.identity.read, identity.policy.read, identity.user.write, identity.group.write, identity.policy.write, identity.host.enroll, identity.host.leave, identity.keytab.rotate, dns.directory.write
Manage local accounts, which are the path to a host that does not go through the directory, and the SSH keys on them.localuser.read, localuser.create, localuser.lock, localuser.unlock, localuser.sshkeys.add, localuser.sshkeys.remove, localuser.sshkeys.replace, localuser.sshkeys.write
Let a scheduled entry run as root. The permission travels with the order rather than standing on its own.schedule.root.exec

What it may not do. It does not grant root by another name: accounts.privileged_groups is withheld from it, as from every role but the administrator, so this role opens ordinary accounts and an account that lands in sudo, wheel, docker or lxd is opened by whoever may grant root. It does not change the groups or the expiry of an account, and does not delete one. It holds schedule.root.exec but not schedule.write, so it cannot write a scheduled entry at all — the permission is one an administrator's order carries. And it does not order operations: job.create is not in the list.

platform_admin

The administrator holds all 187 permissions in the catalogue: every permission the panel defines is in this role, and nothing is outside it. Some of them are in this role alone.

Held by nobody elseWhat it decides

accounts.privileged_groupsAn account created in, or moved into, a group that is root by another name.

Holding every permission is not the same as being unconditional. The administrator meets the second-person rule in a production environment like anybody else, and the operations of the greatest impact ask them for a reason and a fresh authentication — both are described under When a role is not enough.

The scope of a binding

A binding is a role within a scope. The scope has five categories, and a target — a host, or an order that names a place in the fleet — carries the facts the categories are compared against.

CategoryWhat it narrows byHow it is written
siteThe site the host stands in.One value, or *.
environmentThe environment the host belongs to.One value, or *.
teamThe team the host is assigned to.In words: {"mode":"any"} or {"mode":"exact","team_id":"…"}.
ownerThe owner recorded on the host.A list of values, any of which satisfies it, or a list containing *.
tagThe tags on the host.A list of values, any of which satisfies it, or a list containing *.

Two rules decide everything else:

  • Every category has to be satisfied at once. Site and environment and team and owner and tag. A binding that names a site and a tag reaches the hosts that are in that site and carry that tag, not the union of the two.
  • Within a category the values are alternatives. Two owners on one binding mean either of them; two tags mean a host carrying either. A single value is the same rule with one alternative.

An asterisk is the only thing that widens a category: it covers every value, including a target that carries none — an unowned host is still a host. An empty category is not a wide one: it reaches nothing. That is deliberate, and it is the direction a mistake falls in. A binding built field by field with one field forgotten grants nothing at all, rather than quietly granting the fleet.

The team is the one category that cannot say “any” by being empty, because it is a foreign key and has no value meaning all. So it is said in words, and a scope answers about it in words too: {"mode":"any"} or {"mode":"exact","team_id":"…"}. A reader that has to infer any from a missing field is a reader that will one day infer it wrongly.

For the same reason a scope is written out in full wherever it is printed or serialised. In the audit trail it reads site=* env=prod team=* owner=* tag=*, with every category named; over the API every category is a field of the JSON object, never left out. A list with no values at all prints as (none), which is what reaches nothing.

A category the query cannot check is not satisfied

Some lists have no column to compare a category against. An enrollment order carries a site and an environment and nothing else — there is no host yet, so there is no team, no owner and no tags. A binding narrowed by one of those reaches no such order at all, rather than every one of them: answering “everything” where nothing can be checked is how a narrow binding becomes the whole fleet.

The shape of a grant

A binding is granted with POST /api/v1/principals/{id}/roles, and the same shape is carried in the roles list when an identity is created. It needs principal.manage across the whole fleet, and it is an operation of the greatest impact: it moves who can do anything at all.

POST /api/v1/principals/{id}/roles
{
  "role":        "operator",
  "site":        "waw",
  "environment": "prod",
  "team_scope":  {"mode": "any"},
  "owners":      ["*"],
  "tags":        ["*"],
  "valid_until": "2026-12-31T23:59:59Z",
  "reason":      "on-call rotation for the Warsaw site"
}

What the panel refuses:

RefusalWhen
unknown_roleThe role is not one of the six.
invalid_bindingteam_scope is missing. It is required, and the refusal says what to write: {"mode":"any"} or {"mode":"exact","team_id":"…"}. A team omitted by accident would widen the grant in silence.
invalid_bindingteam_scope is malformed: a mode that is neither any nor exact, a team_id given with mode any, or mode exact with no team_id.
invalid_bindingvalid_until is not an RFC 3339 moment. Leaving it out means the binding lasts until somebody revokes it.
scope_escalationThe binding would be wider than the one the caller holds. See below.
reason_requiredThe reason is shorter than 8 characters.
reauthentication_requiredThe caller’s authentication is not fresh enough for an operation of this impact.

A binding over a team has a route of its own, POST /api/v1/principals/{id}/team-roles, which takes the team and nothing else: the scope it stores is the team with an asterisk in every other category. It takes two permissions rather than one — principal.manage and team.binding.write — because binding a role to a team hands over whatever that team holds. A request there naming a site or an environment as well is refused with scope_conflict: a binding names one vocabulary or the other, never both. A team that is not a team identifier is invalid_team, and a team nobody has created is team_not_found — a binding over an identifier that does not exist yet would start granting the moment somebody created a team with that name.

owners and tags left out of the request are read as ["*"]: this is the one place the panel fills a category in, and it does so because a grant request that says nothing about owners means the caller did not narrow by them. The team is not filled in the same way, and a scope stored with an empty category still reaches nothing. A binding is revoked with DELETE /api/v1/principals/{id}/roles/{role}, which names the site and the environment in the body; an identity with no such binding is answered binding_not_found.

A binding may carry valid_until, after which it grants nothing without anybody revoking it. The access review at GET /api/v1/access/review flags an administrator whose binding has no end date at all.

Nobody grants what they do not hold

Before a grant is written the panel takes every permission the role carries and asks whether the caller holds that permission in a scope that contains the one being granted. One permission missing is enough to refuse the whole grant, and the refusal names it:

403 scope_escalation
the role operator carries packages.plan, which you do not hold in
site=waw env=prod team=* owner=* tag=*

The comparison is on what a binding reaches, not on columns that happen to match. A caller bound to one team cannot hand out “any team”; a caller whose binding lists two tags cannot hand out *; an asterisk on the caller’s side contains anything in that category. The attempt is recorded in the audit trail as a denied principal.role.grant or principal.create with reason: scope_escalation and the message above — somebody trying to widen their own access is worth reading afterwards.

A directory group becomes a role

Nobody is given a role by hand in the usual case. A group of the identity provider is mapped to a role within a scope, and whoever signs in carrying that group gets that binding. The mapping is written with POST /api/v1/group-mappings — see the first administrator for the request and the order the steps are taken in.

Three things about the mapping are worth knowing:

  • The group grants a role; the scope stays the panel’s policy. A mapping narrows by site and environment and by nothing else, and it says so in every category: the team is any, the owners and the tags are *. Filling two fields and leaving three empty would grant nothing at all.
  • The mapping is read on every request, so removing one takes the role away on the user’s next request rather than at their next login.
  • A group with no mapping grants nothing. With no mapping at all, whoever signs in through the provider has no permission — which is the single most common reason a fresh installation looks broken.

The laboratory maps five groups, one per role that is used there. They are ordinary directory groups; the names carry no meaning to the panel beyond what the mapping gives them:

GroupRole
flotestro-viewersviewer
flotestro-operatorsoperator
flotestro-prod-approversapprover
flotestro-identity-adminsidentity_admin
flotestro-platform-adminsplatform_admin

Which site and environment each of those mappings narrows by is the installation’s decision and not the group’s: the panel reads the scope from the mapping, never from the name.

The group names reach the panel as the provider writes them. A group mapper that emits full paths — /flotestro-operators — is read the same as one that emits bare names, and a provider that puts a single group in a string rather than a list is read the same as one that sends a list of one.

When a role is not enough

Three things are asked for on top of a permission. None of them can be granted by a role, and none of them replaces one.

A reason and a fresh authentication

The operations of the greatest impact — every change to the access rules among them — require a reason of at least 8 characters and an authentication no older than the installation allows. Every route in this chapter that writes is one of them: creating an identity, granting or revoking a role, issuing or revoking a token, ending a session, disabling or enabling an identity, and writing or removing a group mapping. A reason that is too short is answered 400 reason_required, which signing in again will not fix; an authentication that is too old is answered 401 reauthentication_required. What the panel accepted is recorded with the change itself: the purpose the operator typed, the method the provider reported and the moment of authentication. The settings behind it are in the API chapter.

Operations on hosts have their own version of the same rule, decided by the risk level rather than by the route: a critical or destructive operation requires fresh authentication, and a destructive one also requires the operator to type the target name. The risk of one order can be higher than the level of the operation — an order that creates an account in a privileged group, or moves one into it, is critical whatever the operation says, and so is one that hands over the access of an account the host reported as privileged. The levels and the operations that carry them are in the operations chapter.

A second person

A mutating operation always waits for an approval: the request may ask for an approval where none is needed, never waive one. In a production environment — one named by FLOTESTRO_PRODUCTION_ENVIRONMENTS, prod,production by default — the approval may not come from the person who ordered the change. The attempt is answered 403 self_approval and recorded as a denied job.approve. The same rule holds for a campaign and for a change in the directory.

This is why operator and approver are separate roles rather than one. An identity holding both in the same production environment can order a change and then be refused its approval, which is the rule working rather than a misconfiguration — but it leaves the change waiting for somebody who was not meant to be needed.

A permission the order carries

Three permissions are asked for by the content of an order, on top of the one its operation needs. An order whose payload calls for one of them and whose actor does not hold it is refused, whatever role that actor has.

PermissionWhen the order needs itWho holds it
schedule.root.execA scheduled entry whose user is root.identity_admin, platform_admin
file.write.unvalidatedA file write or rollback that carries allow_missing_validator: the host lacks the validator the order relies on.platform_admin
accounts.privileged_groupsAn order that creates an account in, or moves an account into, a group that is root by another name.platform_admin
